Type Ia supernovae · distance · expansion history

A stellar explosion becomes a ruler for the Universe.

A Type Ia supernova can briefly rival the light of its host galaxy. Correct its light-curve width and colour, compare apparent with standardized luminosity, and it yields a distance. Repeat that measurement across redshift and the changing expansion rate appears in the Hubble diagram.

What is a supernova?

A rapid stellar disruption; a long-lived expanding remnant.

A supernova is observed first as a new point source that brightens and fades over weeks or months. Years later, telescopes may resolve the ejecta and shocks as a remnant. The gallery above deliberately shows both stages.

Ia

Thermonuclear supernova

A carbon–oxygen white dwarf in a binary system undergoes runaway nuclear burning and is disrupted. The light curve is powered principally by the radioactive decay chain 56Ni → 56Co → 56Fe. Type Ia spectra lack hydrogen and show strong silicon near maximum light.

They are standardizable, not perfectly standard: broader, slower-declining light curves are intrinsically brighter, and colour carries luminosity and dust information.

II / Ib / Ic

Core-collapse supernova

A massive star exhausts nuclear fuel, its core collapses, and the outer layers are expelled. SN 1987A, Cassiopeia A, and the Veil Nebula are core-collapse examples included to show the anatomy of ejecta and blast waves—not the Type Ia distance sample used below.

From photons to expansion history

How a supernova enters the Hubble diagram.

  1. 01

    Observe

    Repeated images measure flux versus time in several bands; spectroscopy supplies classification and redshift.

    f(t, λ) → mB, x1, c, z
  2. 02

    Standardize

    Light-curve shape and colour corrections reduce object-to-object luminosity variation.

    mBcorr = mB + αx1 − βc + …
  3. 03

    Infer distance

    The difference between corrected apparent magnitude and standardized absolute magnitude gives distance modulus.

    μ = mcorr − M = 5 log10(dL/Mpc) + 25
  4. 04

    Fit the curve

    The redshift-dependent shape of μ(z), not a sky image, constrains the expansion history and Ωm.

    μ(z) ↔ H(z), Ωm, ΩΛ

Calculation

Four equations drive the browser analysis.

01

Flat ΛCDM geometry

The luminosity distance is evaluated numerically with ΩΛ = 1 − Ωm and no radiation term.

dL = (1 + z)c/H0 ∫ dz′/E(z′)
02

Effective uncertainty

The released diagonal distance-modulus error is combined with the selected intrinsic scatter.

σ²eff,i = σ²diag,i + σ²int
03

Profiled calibration

At every Ωm, the additive magnitude offset is solved analytically, isolating curve shape from absolute scale.

ΔM̂ = Σwi(μi − μmodel,i) / Σwi
04

Likelihood profile

The interval is the grid crossing at Δχ² = 1 under independent Gaussian diagonal errors.

χ² = Σ[(μi − μmodel,i − ΔM̂)/σeff,i]²

Numerical scope

  • Default selection: 168/180 rows at z ≥ 0.010.
  • Default scatter: σint = 0.10 mag, added in quadrature.
  • Fit grid: Ωm = 0.05–1.00 in steps of 0.005.
  • Covariance: diagonal terms only; the official systematic covariance is not loaded.
  • Calibration: ΔM is profiled, so this experiment does not independently measure H0.
